The Leather Care Guide

Cleaning, conditioning, storage and repair — and the well-meant habits that quietly destroy good leather.

Leather is skin. It dries out, it stains, it marks, and it recovers from far more than most people expect. Nearly all the damage we see is caused by three things: too much heat, too much product and too much enthusiasm with water.

Know what you are caring for first

Untreated and full-grain leathers absorb and need feeding. Coated leathers such as saffiano and patent repel and need nothing but a wipe. Suede needs brushing and protecting and hates everything else. Using the wrong routine is worse than using none, so start with the Leather Type Decoder if you are unsure.

Routine cleaning

Empty the bag, turn the lining out if it will come, and shake the crumbs away. Wipe the outside with a soft dry cloth. For marks, use a barely damp cloth, working in small circles, and dry immediately with a second cloth. Never soak leather and never use household cleaners, baby wipes or spirit-based solvents.

Conditioning, and how to not overdo it

Two or three times a year for uncoated leather is plenty. Test on the base first. Apply a small amount with a cloth, not your fingers, in thin even passes, then leave it an hour and buff. If the surface still feels tacky the next day you used too much, which softens the structure and attracts dust.

Storage is where most damage happens

Stuff the bag loosely with acid-free paper or a soft cloth so the shape holds, close the zip halfway, and keep it in its dust bag on a shelf. Not hanging by the handle, which stretches the anchors. Not in plastic, which traps moisture and grows mould. Not stacked under something heavy, which creases the front panel permanently.

Suede and nubuck

Brush after every wear, always in one direction. Protect with a spray made for suede before the first outing and twice a year after. Blot rain, never rub it. Shiny patches can often be lifted with a suede eraser and a brush. Accept that suede shows its life at the corners, because it will.

When to stop and ask for help

Torn stitching, a failed zip, a strap pulling away from its anchor and any deep stain are all repairable, usually for a fraction of the replacement cost, and all of them get worse with home experiments. Find a leather repairer with photographs of their own work, and ask what they would do before agreeing to anything.

A realistic routine

Wipe after a wet day. Brush suede weekly. Empty and air the bag monthly. Condition twice a year. Store it properly every time you stop using it. That is the whole of it, and bags treated that way routinely outlive the people who worried about them.
